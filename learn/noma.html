Orthogonal access and its limit

Every generation up to 4G gave each user its own slice: of time, of frequency, of code. Users never interfere, which keeps things simple. But a slice given to a user with a weak channel carries few bits, and nobody else can use it.

Power-domain NOMA in two steps

Step one, at the base station: add the two users’ signals together, giving the far user more power. Step two, at the near user: decode the loud part first, cancel it, then decode your own.

Key idea

Successive interference cancellation (SIC): decode the strongest signal, subtract it, and repeat. The decoding order follows the channel strength: weaker users are decoded first.

Why it beats orthogonal access

Superposition with SIC reaches the capacity region of the two-user downlink. Every TDMA point lies inside it, and the gap grows with the difference between the users’ channels.

Things to try:

  • Make the two SNRs equal. The curves meet: with identical channels NOMA gains nothing.
  • Separate them by 20 dB or more. For the same near-user rate, the far user gets clearly more with NOMA.
  • Push α towards 0.5. Interference at the far user grows and its rate collapses: power allocation is the main design lever.

Uplink, grant-free access and RIS

In the uplink, users transmit together and the base station runs SIC, strongest first. That needs clearly different received powers. A RIS can create them on purpose.

This matters for grant-free access, where devices send without waiting for a scheduling grant: collisions are unavoidable, and power differences resolve them. This is the idea behind the virtual RIS partitioning and STAR-RIS clustering papers below. RIS tutorial

Beyond NOMA: rate splitting

NOMA asks one user to decode another’s whole message. Rate-splitting multiple access (RSMA) asks for only part of it.

Practical issues

  • Error propagationA wrongly decoded first layer corrupts every layer after it.
  • Imperfect SICResidual interference, made worse by hardware impairments.
  • Pairing and powerWhich users to combine and how to split power, usually with minimum rates.
  • Channel knowledgeThe transmitter needs channel estimates to order users.
  • SecurityThe near user decodes the far user’s message as a matter of course.
  • StandardsStudied for LTE as MUST; RSMA is a 6G candidate.
Key takeaways
  • NOMA superimposes users in power and separates them with successive interference cancellation.
  • In the downlink, the near user decodes and removes the far user’s signal before decoding its own.
  • The gain over orthogonal access grows with the difference between the users’ channels.
  • A RIS can create that difference on purpose, and RSMA generalises the idea.

Check your understanding

In two-user downlink NOMA, which user performs SIC?

The near user, with the stronger channel. It decodes and removes the far user’s signal before decoding its own.

Why does the far user get more power?

Its channel is weaker and it treats the near user’s signal as noise, so it needs the larger share. The larger power also makes the near user’s first SIC step reliable.

Two users have identical channels. Does NOMA beat TDMA in sum rate?

No. Both achieve log₂(1 + SNR), and their rate regions coincide.
