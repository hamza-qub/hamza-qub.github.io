Where the spectrum is

Every generation has moved up in frequency to find more bandwidth. Above 100 GHz lie tens of gigahertz of largely unused spectrum: the sub-terahertz and terahertz bands.

Why bandwidth

Shannon’s formula says rate grows in proportion to bandwidth, but only logarithmically with SNR. Huge bandwidths are the shortest path to huge data rates.

See the communication model tab for where the formula comes from.

The catch: losses

Higher frequencies bring two kinds of loss. Spreading loss grows if antennas keep the same gain. And the air itself absorbs energy at specific frequencies, where oxygen and water molecules resonate.

Key idea

Absorption depends on humidity and distance and is computed with ITU-R P.676. For short indoor links, spreading loss and blockage matter far more.

Antennas win it back

The free-space loss formula assumes antennas of fixed gain, which shrink as the wavelength shrinks. Keep the antennas the same physical size instead, and the link gets better at higher frequency.

Pencil beams and blockage

Very narrow beams carry the link, so they must be found, pointed and tracked. And almost anything in the way blocks them.

What it will be used for

Wireless backhaulFibre-like point-to-point links between base stations.
Data centresRack-to-rack and chip-to-chip links at hundreds of gigabits per second.
Instant downloadsShort-range kiosks that transfer gigabytes in seconds.
Imaging and sensingCentimetre-level resolution for ISAC, security scanning and quality control.
Key takeaways
  • The sub-THz and THz bands offer tens of gigahertz of bandwidth, the most direct route to very high data rates.
  • Oxygen and water vapour absorb at specific frequencies; links use the windows between them.
  • For antennas of fixed size, the link improves with frequency, at the cost of very narrow beams.
  • Blockage, beam alignment and hardware limits are the main obstacles.

Check your understanding

Why do higher frequencies offer more bandwidth?

Bandwidth is roughly proportional to the carrier frequency for a given fractional bandwidth, and the spectrum above 100 GHz is largely unallocated.

Is free-space path loss really higher at 300 GHz than at 3 GHz?

Only for antennas of fixed gain. With antennas of fixed physical size, the link improves by 40 dB between the two frequencies.

Why are terahertz links usually short?

Absorption grows with distance, transmitters produce little power, and narrow beams are easily blocked.
