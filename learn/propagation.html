Wireless propagation

Between two antennas, a radio wave spreads out, bounces, bends and scatters. The result is a channel that loses power with distance and fades as you move.

Waves spread out

A transmitted wave spreads in every direction, so the power that reaches a receiver of fixed size falls with distance even in empty space.

Key idea

Free-space path loss between isotropic antennas, with d in metres and f in hertz:

It comes from the Friis equation, FSPL = (4πd/λ)². At 3.5 GHz the loss is 43 dB at 1 m and 103 dB at 1 km.

Real environments lose more

On the ground, the signal is blocked, reflected and absorbed on the way. Engineers capture this with a single number, the path-loss exponent n: power falls as 1/dⁿ instead of 1/d².

  • openn ≈ 2Free space, and line of sight in open areas.
  • cityn ≈ 2.7 to 3.5Urban cellular links.
  • shadowedn ≈ 3 to 5Shadowed urban areas.
  • indoorsn ≈ 4 to 6Indoors, through walls and floors.

Reflection, diffraction, scattering

Three mechanisms carry signals where there is no line of sight. Every real channel is a mix of all three.

Multipath: many copies of one signal

The receiver gets the signal several times, along paths of different length. Each copy arrives with its own delay, strength and phase.

Key idea

The coherence bandwidth, roughly the inverse of the delay spread, is the range of frequencies over which the channel looks the same. Signals narrower than it fade flat; wider signals are frequency selective.

Fading at three scales

Received power varies on three very different scales: the average trend over kilometres, shadowing over tens of metres, and fast fading over fractions of a wavelength.

With a direct path of power times the scattered power, the amplitude is Rician. The Nakagami-m distribution is a flexible fit to both, and is used throughout the performance analysis tab.

Try it: how far does a signal reach?

Change the frequency, the environment and the shadowing, and see where the signal drops below what a typical receiver can decode.

Things to try:

  • Move from 3.5 GHz to 28 GHz. With the same isotropic antennas, the extra 18 dB of loss shrinks the range about four times for n = 3.
  • At 3.5 GHz, raise n from 2 to 4. The range falls from about 7 km to under 100 m.
  • Increase the shadowing. The average range stays the same, but the walk dips below the line much earlier, and the margin needed for reliable coverage grows.

Small-scale fading is left out here; it would add rapid dips of 10 to 30 dB on top. The link budget tab shows how engineers leave margins for both.

Key takeaways
  • Free-space loss grows 20 dB per decade of distance; real environments are captured by a path-loss exponent n between 2 and 6.
  • Reflection, diffraction and scattering create multipath. Diffraction weakens at high frequencies, so millimetre waves are easily blocked.
  • The delay spread decides whether a channel is flat or frequency selective across the band.
  • Power varies on three scales: path loss, shadowing and fast fading, each needing its own margin or technique.

Check your understanding

A receiver moves from 200 m to 400 m from the transmitter in free space. How much power does it lose?

Doubling the distance costs 6 dB.

Why does a 28 GHz signal struggle more than a 3.5 GHz signal behind a building corner?

Diffraction around edges weakens as the wavelength gets shorter, and millimetre waves also lose more passing through walls, so shadowed areas receive far less.

A channel has a delay spread of 2 µs. Will a 20 MHz signal see flat fading?

No. The coherence bandwidth is roughly 1/(5 × 2 µs) = 100 kHz, much narrower than 20 MHz, so the channel is frequency selective.
