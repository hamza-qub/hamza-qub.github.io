Link budgets

A link budget adds up every gain and loss between two radios to answer one question: will the signal arrive strong enough to beat the noise?

Decibels: the language of links

Powers in a wireless link span more than 20 orders of magnitude. Engineers use decibels so they can add and subtract instead of multiplying.

Key idea

In decibels, a link budget is just a sum: start from the transmit power, add every gain, subtract every loss.

Noise sets the floor

A receiver is only as good as its noise. Thermal noise grows with bandwidth, and the receiver’s own electronics add a little more, measured by the noise figure.

More detail: where −174 comes from

The −174 dBm/Hz is : Boltzmann’s constant, J/K, times a room temperature of 290 K, expressed in dBm per hertz. A phone’s noise figure is typically 5 to 9 dB; a base station’s, 2 to 5 dB.

From power to data rate

Subtract the noise from the received power and you have the SNR. Put that into Shannon’s formula and you have the best possible data rate.

Things to try:

  • Switch from 20 MHz to 400 MHz at 28 GHz. The noise rises 13 dB, but the rate can still grow because there is so much more bandwidth.
  • At 28 GHz, raise the antenna gains to 35 dBi. Large arrays at both ends win back the extra path loss; this is how millimetre-wave 5G works.
  • Find the distance where the SNR crosses 0 dB. Beyond it, the link still works, but only at low rates.

Margins: designing for bad days

The budget so far is for an average day. Fading and shadowing make the signal dip, so designers add margins: extra decibels held in reserve.

  • slowShadowing marginAbout 1.28σ for 90% coverage at the cell edge: 8 dB when σ = 6 dB.
  • fastFade margin10 to 30 dB against fast fading, unless diversity or coding handles it.
  • localBody and entry lossA hand, a head or a wall can cost 3 to 20 dB, more at high frequencies.
Key takeaways
  • In decibels, a link budget is a sum: transmit power plus gains minus losses minus margins.
  • Path loss is by far the largest term, typically 100 to 150 dB for cellular links.
  • Noise power is −174 dBm/Hz plus 10·log₁₀ B plus the noise figure, so wider bands bring more noise.
  • Margins of several to tens of decibels protect against shadowing and fading.

Check your understanding

What is 2 W in dBm?

10·log₁₀(2000 mW) ≈ 33 dBm.

A receiver has a 9 dB noise figure and a 100 MHz bandwidth. What is its noise floor?

−174 + 80 + 9 = −85 dBm.

A link has 15 dB of SNR to spare in Rayleigh fading. Roughly how reliable is it?

About 15 dB of fade margin corresponds to an outage probability of about 3%, so it works roughly 97% of the time.
