Echoes and delay

A radar is a transmitter and a receiver that listens for its own signal. Send a pulse, wait for the echo, and time the gap: radio travels at the speed of light, so delay is distance.

Key idea

Range comes from delay, velocity from the Doppler shift, angle from phase differences across antennas. Each is limited by one resource:

Why echoes are so weak

A communication signal crosses the distance once. A radar signal crosses it twice and is scattered by the target on the way, so its power falls as the fourth power of range.

Pulse compression and the matched filter

A short pulse gives fine range resolution but little energy. A long pulse gives energy but blurs the range. The fix: send a long pulse whose frequency sweeps across a wide band, then compress it at the receiver.

Key idea

The matched filter correlates the received signal with a copy of the transmitted one. It maximises the SNR for a known signal in white noise, and the gain equals the time–bandwidth product .

The range–Doppler map

Modern radars, including automotive ones, send a burst of chirps (FMCW). One FFT along each chirp gives range; a second FFT across the chirps gives the Doppler shift. The result is a picture of the scene by distance and speed.

More detail: FMCW and ambiguity

In FMCW, mixing the echo with the transmitted chirp gives a beat tone at for chirp slope , so range becomes a frequency. Across chirps spaced apart, a target’s phase rotates by per chirp, which the Doppler FFT turns into velocity.

Detection: CFAR

Which bright cells are targets? A fixed threshold fails, because the noise and clutter level changes across the map. CFAR, constant false-alarm rate detection, sets the threshold from each cell’s neighbourhood.

Things to try:

  • Lower the weak target’s SNR until CFAR misses it. Then relax the false-alarm probability to 10⁻² and watch it come back, along with some false alarms.
  • Beyond cell 150 the noise rises by 8 dB. The threshold follows it, which a fixed threshold could not.
  • Reduce the training cells to 4. The noise estimate becomes noisy, so α must grow to keep the same false-alarm rate.

Finding the angle

An array sees an echo arrive at slightly different times on each antenna. Scanning a beam across angles finds the direction, but two targets closer than a beamwidth merge. Subspace methods such as MUSIC split them.

Where radar is used

  • Automotive76–81 GHz FMCW radars for cruise control, braking and parking; 4D imaging radars add elevation.
  • WeatherDoppler radars map rain and wind; dual polarisation tells rain from hail.
  • Air traffic and defenceLong-range surveillance with electronically steered phased arrays.
  • Health and gesturesmmWave radars sense breathing, heartbeat and hand gestures without contact.
Key takeaways
  • Range comes from echo delay, velocity from Doppler, angle from phase across an array.
  • Echo power falls as 1/r⁴, so radars need high gain and long integration.
  • Pulse compression gets fine resolution (1/B) from a long, energetic pulse through the matched filter.
  • FMCW radars build range–Doppler maps with two FFTs; CFAR picks out targets; array processing finds their angles.

Check your understanding

A radar pulse returns after 2 µs. How far is the target?

r = cτ/2 = 3×10⁸ × 2×10⁻⁶ / 2 = 300 m.

Why does a radar link lose 12 dB, not 6 dB, when range doubles?

The echo power falls as 1/r⁴: 2⁴ = 16, which is 12 dB.

Why can’t a fixed threshold detect targets reliably?

The noise and clutter level varies across the map; a fixed threshold gives many false alarms where it is high and misses where it is low. CFAR adapts to the local level.

What sets the velocity resolution of an FMCW radar?

The total observation time across the burst of chirps: Δv = λ/(2Tobs).
