Integrated sensing and communication

ISAC uses the same spectrum, hardware and often the same signal to send data and to sense the surroundings, which turns the network into a radar.

Why combine them

Radar and communication grew up as separate systems with separate spectrum. Their hardware has converged since: both use wide bandwidths, large antenna arrays and digital signal processing, and both increasingly work at millimetre-wave frequencies. Sharing spectrum and hardware saves both. A network that can sense is also useful in its own right. It can track vehicles and drones, detect people, map indoor spaces, and use what it senses to point its own beams.

Integrated sensing and communication (ISAC) is one of the six usage scenarios in IMT-2030, the ITU framework for 6G. See the 6G tutorial.

Levels of integration

Coexistence
Separate radar and communication signals share spectrum and manage the interference between them.
Co-design
Separate signals are designed together, for example by sharing time slots, frequencies or beams.
Dual-function
One waveform does both jobs. A base station sends a data signal and listens for its echoes.

Geometry matters too. In monostatic sensing the transmitter and the sensing receiver sit together, so the receiver must listen for weak echoes while its own transmitter is on. That needs full-duplex isolation or cancellation. In bistatic sensing they are apart, which removes the leakage but requires tight synchronisation between sites.

The trade-off

Sensing and communication want different things from a signal. Sensing prefers known waveforms with sharp autocorrelation, and beams that sweep a scene or point at targets. Communication needs random, information-bearing symbols and beams pointed at users. In a dual-function design these goals compete for the same power, time, bandwidth and beam directions.

The best achievable combinations of sensing accuracy, for example the Cramér–Rao bound on the estimation error, and communication rate form a Pareto boundary. Switching in time between a pure radar mode and a pure communication mode stays inside that boundary. Joint designs can reach it.

Sensing for security

A broadcast signal reaches eavesdroppers as well as legitimate users. Physical-layer security tries to keep the legitimate link better than the eavesdropper’s, which is measured by the secrecy rate

where and are the SNRs at the user and the eavesdropper. A common tool is jamming: sending artificial noise towards the eavesdropper. That only works if you know where the eavesdropper is, and usually you do not.

Sensing can fill that gap. An ISAC base station can estimate the position of a suspicious target from its echoes and then aim jamming at that region while keeping the user’s link clean. The position estimate has errors, so the jamming has to be robust to them, covering an uncertainty region rather than a single point. This is the sense-and-jam idea in the Asilomar 2024 paper listed below.

Open problems

  • Self-interference in monostatic operation.
  • Waveform choice. OFDM suits communication and works for sensing, but its peak-to-average power ratio and sidelobes cost performance.
  • Synchronisation of clocks between sites in bistatic and multistatic sensing.
  • Privacy. A network that can sense people raises questions of consent and data protection.
  • Metrics. Comparing detection probability, estimation error and data rate on a common scale.
Key takeaways
  • ISAC shares spectrum, hardware and sometimes the waveform between sensing and communication.
  • Range resolution is , so wide bandwidths make good sensors.
  • Sensing and communication compete for the same resources. Joint designs reach a Pareto boundary that time sharing cannot.
  • Sensing can support security: locate the threat, then jam it.

Why is monostatic ISAC harder to build than bistatic?

The transmitter and receiver sit together, so the strong transmitted signal leaks into the receiver while it listens for weak echoes. This needs full-duplex isolation or cancellation.

Why must a sensing-assisted jammer be robust?

The eavesdropper’s estimated position has errors. Jamming aimed at a single point can miss, so it has to cover the region where the eavesdropper could be.
