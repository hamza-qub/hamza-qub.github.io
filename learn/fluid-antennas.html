Fluid antenna systems

A fluid antenna can change its position within a small space. The wireless channel changes over a fraction of a wavelength, so that freedom buys diversity without extra radio chains.

Antennas that move

Multipath propagation makes the received signal strength vary from point to point. Two spots half a wavelength apart can see very different channels, one in a deep fade and the other near a peak. A conventional antenna stays where it was installed and accepts whatever fade it sits in.

A fluid antenna system (FAS) can move its radiating element within a small linear or planar space. The idea was first described with liquid metal flowing in a tube. The term now covers any antenna whose position or shape can change, including pixel-reconfigurable antennas that switch electronically between many closely spaced ports. Movable antennas, driven by motors, are a closely related idea.

In the usual model a FAS has one radio-frequency chain and possible positions, called ports, spread over a length of wavelengths. It switches to the best port.

  • Increase instead. A longer antenna keeps adding diversity.
  • Set and press New channel a few times. The better of two ports is often far better than the first one, which is the whole case for fluid antennas.

Multiple access without precoding

Fluid antenna multiple access (FAMA) uses the same mechanism to separate users. Interference fades along the antenna just as the wanted signal does. Each user switches to the port where its own signal is strong relative to the interference, so many users can share a channel without the base station knowing their channels or computing precoders. Fast FAMA changes port with every symbol; slow FAMA changes once per channel coherence time.

Compact ultra-massive antenna arrays (CUMA) extend this by activating many ports at once and combining those whose wanted signals add up constructively, while the interference adds incoherently. Port selection for CUMA is the subject of a recent preprint listed below.

Fluid RIS moves the idea from the user’s antenna to the surface: RIS elements whose positions can also be chosen add a spatial degree of freedom to the phase shifts. Two 2026 letters listed below analyse fluid-RIS-enabled NOMA.

Open problems

  • Channel estimation. Choosing a port needs channel knowledge at many ports, but one radio chain can measure only one port at a time.
  • Switching speed. Liquid and mechanical designs move slowly; electronic pixel antennas switch fast but add loss.
  • Realistic models. Mutual coupling between ports, and correlation beyond ideal uniform scattering.
  • Joint designs with RIS, NOMA and ISAC.
Key takeaways
  • A FAS chooses its antenna position among ports in a space of wavelengths, with a single radio chain.
  • Port correlation follows , so about independent ports’ worth of diversity is available.
  • FAMA separates users by port selection alone. CUMA and fluid RIS extend the idea.

Check your understanding

Why does adding more ports to an antenna of fixed length bring diminishing returns?

The ports end up close together and their channels become highly correlated, so each new port adds little new information.

What does FAMA need from the base station?

Nothing beyond normal transmission. The base station sends without precoding, and each user’s antenna picks the port with the best signal-to-interference ratio.
