Vehicular communications

V2X connects vehicles to each other, to the roadside, to pedestrians and to the network. Speed is what makes it hard: the channel can change in a fraction of a millisecond.

The four links

Vehicle-to-everything (V2X) communication covers four kinds of link.

V2V, vehicle to vehicle
Collision warnings, platooning and cooperative manoeuvres between nearby cars.
V2I, vehicle to infrastructure
Roadside units at junctions, traffic lights and toll points.
V2P, vehicle to pedestrian
Phones and wearables of people near the road.
V2N, vehicle to network
The cellular network, for traffic information, maps, software updates and entertainment.

Two families of standards

IEEE 802.11p adapted Wi-Fi for vehicles. It is the basis of DSRC in the United States and ITS-G5 in Europe, and IEEE 802.11bd is its successor.

Cellular V2X arrived as LTE-V2X in 3GPP Release 14 and as NR-V2X in Release 16, which added unicast and groupcast sidelinks for advanced use cases. In NR-V2X, sidelink resources are either scheduled by the base station (mode 1) or chosen by the vehicles themselves (mode 2), which keeps the sidelink working outside network coverage. Both families mostly use the 5.9 GHz band reserved for intelligent transport systems.

The most demanding use cases, such as cooperative driving, call for latencies of a few milliseconds and reliabilities up to 99.999%, as set out in 3GPP TS 22.186.

Blockage, RIS and drones

Vehicles are themselves obstacles. A lorry between two cars can block the line of sight for seconds, and at millimetre-wave frequencies a blocked link can lose 20 dB or more. Roadside RIS panels can create paths around the obstacle, and two surfaces in sequence can reach users that a single reflection cannot. An aerial RIS on a drone adds height and line of sight to both ends, at the cost of power, flight time and stability.

A RIS can also protect vehicular links from eavesdroppers by focusing energy on the legitimate vehicle and away from others. The papers listed below study dual-RIS vehicle-to-infrastructure links, RIS-aided security for vehicular NOMA and aerial RIS deployment.

Open problems

  • Channel tracking at high speed, increasingly by predicting the channel instead of measuring it.
  • Resource allocation in sidelink mode 2, without central coordination.
  • Latency and reliability together, at the same time and on the same link.
  • Security and privacy of messages that broadcast position and speed.
  • Sensing. Vehicles already carry radars, and the network could sense too. See ISAC.
Key takeaways
  • V2X covers V2V, V2I, V2P and V2N links, over the sidelink (PC5) and the cellular interface (Uu).
  • Doppler shift grows with speed and carrier frequency, and coherence time falls well below a millisecond at millimetre wave.
  • Out-of-date channel knowledge limits every technique that depends on it.
  • Roadside and aerial RIS can route around vehicle blockage.

Check your understanding

A car drives at 90 km/h. What is the maximum Doppler shift at 3.5 GHz?

m/s, so Hz.

Why does the coherence time matter for RIS-assisted V2X?

RIS phases are computed from channel estimates. If the channel changes before the surface is reconfigured, the reflected paths no longer add in phase and the gain drops.

Which 3GPP interface carries direct vehicle-to-vehicle links?

The sidelink, PC5.
