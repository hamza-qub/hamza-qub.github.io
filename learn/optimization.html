Optimization for wireless networks

Every transmitter has to decide how much power to send, where to point its beams and whom to serve. Optimization is the language for making those decisions well, and for proving that they are.

Why wireless needs optimization

A base station has a fixed power budget, a fixed band and many users who want more than it can give. It must split power across users and subcarriers, choose beamforming vectors for its antennas, set the phase of every element of a reconfigurable intelligent surface, decide the decoding order in NOMA, and decide which users to serve at all. Each choice affects every user through interference, so the choices cannot be made one at a time.

Optimization turns this into a precise question: which choice of these variables gives the best value of a performance measure, such as total data rate, the weakest user’s rate or energy efficiency, while respecting the physical limits? Good answers raise capacity at no hardware cost, and the methods for finding them are a large part of 5G and 6G research.

Anatomy of a problem

Every optimization problem has the same three parts. The general form is

  • Variables x: what the system controls, such as transmit powers, beamformers, RIS phases or user-to-cluster assignments.
  • Objective f₀: what we want to minimise, such as transmit power, or the negative of a rate we want to maximise.
  • Constraints f₁, …, fₘ: what must hold, such as a power budget, a minimum rate (quality of service) for each user, or the unit-modulus condition on a passive RIS element.

The points that satisfy all constraints form the feasible set. A problem can be infeasible: if every user demands a high rate from a small power budget, no choice of powers satisfies them all, and recognising this is part of the design.

Convex problems: the easy case

A function is convex if the straight line between any two points on its graph lies on or above the graph:

A problem is convex when the objective and every inequality constraint are convex and the equality constraints are linear. Convex problems have one decisive property: every local minimum is a global minimum. An algorithm that keeps going downhill cannot be trapped.

Convex problems come in a hierarchy of standard classes, from linear programs (LP) to quadratic programs (QP), second-order cone programs (SOCP) and semidefinite programs (SDP). Interior-point methods solve all of them reliably in polynomial time, and modelling tools such as CVX (MATLAB) and CVXPY (Python) let you write the problem almost as you would on paper. Minimum-power beamforming with a target signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratio (SINR) for each user, for example, looks nonconvex at first sight but can be rewritten exactly as an SOCP. Much of the skill in this field is spotting such hidden convexity.

Duality and water-filling

Each constraint can be given a price, called a Lagrange multiplier, and the problem rewritten as a trade-off between the objective and those prices. This dual view gives lower bounds, simple optimality checks (the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker, or KKT, conditions) and, often, closed-form solutions.

Strong channels, with a low floor, get more power, and channels whose floor sits above the water level get none. The water level is found by a simple one-dimensional search.

Where the difficulty comes from

Water-filling is easy because the channels do not interfere. As soon as users share the same band, each user’s rate depends on everyone else’s power:

Here gkk is the gain of user k’s own link and gkj the gain of the interfering link from transmitter j. Maximising this sum rate is nonconvex, and for many users it is NP-hard in general. Nonconvexity in wireless problems usually comes from one of four sources:

  • Interference coupling. Powers or beamformers appear in both the numerator and the denominator of SINRs, as above.
  • Hardware constraints. Passive RIS elements and analog phase shifters can only change phase, so . The set of such points is a circle, not a convex region.
  • Discrete decisions. User association, scheduling, NOMA clustering and fluid-antenna port selection are yes-or-no choices.
  • Ratios. Energy efficiency, in bits per joule, divides a rate by a power and is not concave in general.

Since there is no general method that finds the global optimum of such problems quickly, research focuses on methods that are fast, provably improve at each step, and converge to a point that satisfies the optimality conditions, then shows by simulation that this point performs well.

Successive convex approximation

Successive convex approximation (SCA) is the most widely used of these methods. It replaces the hard problem with a sequence of easy ones. The sum rate above can be written as a difference of two concave functions, R(p) = f(p) − g(p), where f collects the logarithms of signal plus interference plus noise and g the logarithms of interference plus noise. The awkward part is the −g term. Since g is concave, it lies below its tangent at any point, so replacing g by its tangent at the current point p(t) gives a concave function that never exceeds the true rate:

The surrogate touches the true rate at p(t) and lies below it elsewhere. Maximising this concave surrogate is a convex problem. Its solution p(t+1) cannot be worse than p(t), so the rate rises at every step. Since it is bounded by the power budget, it converges, and under mild conditions the limit satisfies the KKT conditions of the original problem. It is a stationary point, not necessarily the global optimum.

Things to try:

  • Press Next iteration a few times. The orange surrogate always touches the blue curve at the current point and lies below it everywhere else. Each step moves to the surrogate’s peak, and after about eight steps SCA settles at the single peak near a 53% split.
  • Try other starting points with the interference still at 0.02. The rate has only one peak, so SCA reaches it from anywhere.
  • Raise the interference to 0.15 and keep the start at 5%. SCA now moves the other way and stops with all power given to user 2: a local optimum, 1.3 bit/s/Hz below the best. Move the start to 90% and it finds the better solution, all power to user 1. With strong interference the best choice is often to switch the weaker link off.

The last case is why papers run SCA from several starting points, or start from a good heuristic, and compare against a global benchmark on small problems.

The wider toolkit

SCA is one of a family of methods. Most papers combine two or three of them.

From model to paper

A journal paper is judged as much on what surrounds the algorithm as on the algorithm itself. Reviewers expect three things:

  • Convergence. Show that the objective improves at every iteration and is bounded, then state what kind of point the method reaches. Claim a global optimum only when it is proved.
  • Complexity. Count the cost per iteration, often from the standard interior-point bounds for the convex subproblem, and compare it with the alternatives.
  • Fair baselines. Compare with a strong existing method, simple heuristics such as random RIS phases, and, on small cases, an exhaustive search or upper bound that shows how close the method gets to the best possible.

Optimization and learning

Iterative methods can be too slow when channels change every millisecond, as in vehicular networks. Learning-based methods train a neural network offline to map channels directly to decisions, so that online use costs one forward pass. Deep unfolding turns the iterations of an algorithm such as WMMSE into the layers of a network. Graph neural networks handle a varying number of users. Deep reinforcement learning suits sequential decisions such as UAV trajectories. These methods still rely on the classical ones: the loss function is usually the optimization objective, and the classical algorithm is the benchmark they have to match.

Key takeaways
  • An optimization problem has variables, an objective and constraints; wireless design is full of them.
  • Convex problems can be solved reliably, because every local optimum is global.
  • Duality and the KKT conditions give closed-form solutions such as water-filling.
  • Interference, unit-modulus hardware, discrete choices and ratios make most real problems nonconvex.
  • SCA and related methods solve a sequence of convex surrogates, improving at each step and converging to a stationary point, which is not always the global optimum.

Check your understanding

Why does a convex problem never trap a descent algorithm in a poor solution?

Because every local minimum of a convex function over a convex set is a global minimum. If the algorithm cannot improve locally, it has already reached the best value.

In water-filling, which channels receive no power?

Those whose noise-to-gain ratio σ²/gₙ is above the water level μ. Their channels are so weak that the power is worth more elsewhere.

Why is maximising the sum rate of interfering users nonconvex, when maximising one user’s rate is not?

Each user’s power appears in its own SINR numerator and in the other users’ denominators. Raising one power helps one user and hurts the others, which bends the sum rate into a shape with several peaks.

SCA has converged. Is the result the global optimum?

Not necessarily. SCA guarantees a monotonically improving objective and, under mild conditions, a stationary (KKT) point. The interactive model shows a case where it stops at a local optimum; multiple starting points or a global benchmark on small cases help check the result.

Why is the constraint |θₙ| = 1 on a RIS element nonconvex?

The set of complex numbers with unit magnitude is a circle. The midpoint of two points on a circle lies inside it, not on it, so the set is not convex.
