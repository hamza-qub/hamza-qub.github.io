Antennas

Antennas turn currents into radio waves and decide where the energy goes. Put many together and you can steer a beam without moving anything.

What an antenna does

An antenna turns an electrical current into a radio wave, and a radio wave back into a current. The same antenna works the same way in both directions; this is called reciprocity.

Key idea

An antenna doesn’t create power. It decides where the power goes. Sending more in one direction means sending less in others.

A family of antennas

Each shape trades size, bandwidth and how tightly it focuses energy.

  • omniDipole≈ 2 dBi. Wide coverage, in phones, routers and as array elements.
  • flatPatch≈ 6 to 9 dBi. Flat and printable, the building block of 5G panels.
  • directionalYagi-Uda≈ 7 to 15 dBi. TV and amateur radio.
  • very directionalHorn and dish≈ 15 to 70 dBi. Satellite, backhaul and deep-space links.

Patterns, gain and beamwidth

An antenna’s radiation pattern shows how strongly it sends in each direction. Its gain compares the strongest direction with an imaginary antenna that radiates equally everywhere.

Small wavelengths, big arrays

An antenna collects power over an effective aperture, an area. At higher frequencies each element is smaller, so the same panel holds many more of them.

Polarization

Radio waves have a direction: the orientation of their electric field. Antennas pick up the matching polarization best.

Matching: the Smith chart

An antenna only radiates the power it accepts. If its impedance differs from the 50 Ω line that feeds it, part of the signal bounces back. The Smith chart maps every possible impedance onto one circle, so engineers can see the mismatch and design the fix.

Arrays steer beams

Feed each element of an array with a slightly delayed copy of the signal, and the waves add up in a chosen direction. Change the delays and the beam moves, with nothing mechanical.

Things to try:

  • Double N from 8 to 16. The beam halves in width and the gain rises by 3 dB.
  • Steer to 50°. The beam gets wider: the array looks shorter from that angle.
  • Increase the spacing beyond 0.5 λ while steering. Copies of the beam, called grating lobes, appear in the wrong directions.

EIRP grows as N², not N: one factor of N from adding the amplifiers’ power, another from focusing it into a narrower beam.

Arrays are the starting point for reconfigurable intelligent surfaces, fluid antennas and sensing.

Key takeaways
  • An antenna converts current to radio waves and back, with the same pattern either way.
  • Gain measures focus relative to an isotropic antenna; it is directivity times efficiency.
  • At higher frequencies, a panel of the same size holds many more elements and so offers more gain.
  • Phase shifts across an array steer its beam; spacing above half a wavelength creates grating lobes.

Check your understanding

A 64-element array replaces a single element. Roughly how much array gain does it add?

10·log₁₀ 64 ≈ 18 dB.

Why does a half-wave dipole not radiate along its own axis?

The current along the wire produces fields perpendicular to it. Seen from the end of the wire, the contributions cancel.

Why do arrays usually space their elements half a wavelength apart?

Wider spacing creates grating lobes when the beam is steered; much closer spacing increases coupling between elements without adding aperture.
