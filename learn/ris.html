Reconfigurable intelligent surfaces

Thin panels of many small elements, each able to switch how it reflects. Together they let the network shape the channel, which engineers could once only measure.

The idea in 30 seconds

A programmable mirrorEach element shifts the phase of what it reflects.
N² gainAligned reflections make the SNR grow with the square of the element count.
Simple hardwareTwo or three bits of phase capture nearly all the gain.

A mirror you can program

A wireless link has two ends you can design and a channel you normally accept as it is. A reconfigurable intelligent surface (RIS) lets the network engineer the channel too.

Inside a surface

A RIS is a thin panel of many small elements, usually half a wavelength apart or less. Each element reflects the incoming wave with a phase that a controller can switch.

  • passiveNo amplifiersNo radio-frequency chains or power amplifiers: it redirects power, it doesn’t create it.
  • hardwareThin and cheapPrinted-circuit technology, mountable on walls, windows and ceilings.
  • controlFast controlPhases change in microseconds, fast enough to follow slowly moving users.

Why phase alignment pays

Each element adds one path. With random phases, the paths partly cancel. When the surface aligns them at the user, they all add up.

Key idea

Two factors of N: a bigger surface collects more of the incoming energy, and coherent alignment focuses it on the user. That is why RIS can overcome its own handicap, the double path loss of going to the surface and back out.

Variants

The basic idea has grown into a family of designs.

  • 360° coverageSTAR-RISTransmits and reflects at once, splitting power to serve users on both sides.
  • amplifiedActive RISA low-power amplifier per element fights double path loss, at the cost of power and noise.
  • airborneAerial RISCarried by a drone or high platform, it can see both ends; placement becomes a design variable.
  • sharedPartitioned RISSub-surfaces serve different users or clusters, useful for NOMA and grant-free access.
  • reconfigurableFluid RISElements that can also move add a spatial degree of freedom.

Open problems

  • Channel estimationA passive surface can’t measure anything, and the number of channels grows with N.
  • HardwarePhase-dependent amplitude, coupling between elements, calibration errors.
  • ControlConfiguring thousands of elements fast enough for moving users.
  • DeploymentWhere to place surfaces, and who owns and powers them.
Key takeaways
  • A RIS reflects with controllable phase; it has no radio chains and creates no new power.
  • Each reflected path suffers double path loss, but coherent combining gives an SNR that grows as N² in the far field.
  • Two or three bits of phase resolution capture nearly all of the gain.
  • STAR, active, aerial, partitioned and fluid surfaces extend the basic idea.

Check your understanding

A 256-element RIS is replaced by a 1,024-element one. With dominant reflected paths in the far field, how does the SNR change?

N grows four times, so the SNR grows sixteen times, about 12 dB.

Why does a 1-bit surface produce a second beam?

With phases of only 0 and π, every element weight is +1 or −1. A real-valued weighting reflects symmetrically about the surface normal, so energy splits between the target angle and its mirror image.

Why is a RIS usually cheaper and lower-power than a relay?

It has no radio-frequency chains or power amplifiers. Its elements only apply phase shifts, so power goes to the control circuitry alone.
