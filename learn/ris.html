Reconfigurable intelligent surfaces

A RIS is a thin panel of many small elements, each able to change how it reflects radio waves. Together they let a network shape the wireless channel, which engineers could once only measure.

The idea

A wireless link has two ends you can design, the transmitter and the receiver, and a channel in between that you normally accept as it is. Walls, buildings and vegetation scatter, absorb and block the signal. A reconfigurable intelligent surface (RIS) is a way to engineer the middle as well.

A RIS is a flat array of small elements, usually spaced half a wavelength apart or less. Each element reflects the incoming wave with a phase shift that a controller can change, typically through PIN diodes or varactors. The surface has no power amplifiers and no radio-frequency chains. It cannot create new signal power; it redirects what arrives. That makes it thin, cheap and frugal compared with a relay.

Variants

STAR-RIS
A simultaneously transmitting and reflecting surface splits the incident power between a reflected wave and a transmitted wave, so one surface serves users on both sides of it. Energy splitting, mode switching and time switching are its three operating protocols.
Active RIS
Each element gets a low-power amplifier to fight the double path loss, at the cost of power consumption and added noise.
Aerial RIS
A surface carried by a drone or mounted on a high platform can see both ends of the link. Its placement becomes a design variable alongside its phases.
Partitioned RIS
One surface is divided into sub-surfaces that serve different users or clusters. Partitioning creates controlled differences between users’ channels, which is useful for NOMA and grant-free access.
Fluid RIS
Elements whose positions can also change add a spatial degree of freedom to the phase shifts. See fluid antenna systems.

Open problems

  • Channel estimation. A passive surface cannot measure anything, and the number of cascaded channel coefficients grows with .
  • Hardware. Amplitude that depends on phase, coupling between neighbouring elements, coarse phase resolution and calibration errors. See hardware impairments.
  • Control. Configuring thousands of elements quickly enough for moving users.
  • Deployment. Where to place surfaces, and who owns and powers them.
  • Near-field operation. Large surfaces at short range need spherical-wave models instead of plane waves.
Key takeaways
  • A RIS reflects with controllable phase. It has no radio chains and creates no new power.
  • Each reflected path suffers double path loss, but coherent combining over elements gives an SNR that grows as in the far field.
  • Two or three bits of phase resolution capture nearly all of the gain.
  • STAR, active, aerial, partitioned and fluid surfaces extend the basic idea.

Why is a RIS usually cheaper and lower-power than a relay?

It has no radio-frequency chains or power amplifiers. Its elements only apply phase shifts, so power goes to the control circuitry alone.
